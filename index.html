Philosophy

Localisation has to mean something, or it's just paperwork.

Too much of what gets called localisation is administrative relocation dressed up as empowerment: the reporting burden moves south, the funding rules stay exactly where they were, and the people doing the actual work absorb the risk without ever holding the budget or the veto.

Genuine ownership looks different. It means a local partner can set the agenda, not just deliver against someone else's logframe. It means they can say no to a donor's terms and the relationship survives it. My research and my field work both start from that distinction, and I don't call something local ownership unless the power actually moved with it, not just the paperwork.

At the same time, I don't treat local actors as passive until a donor grants them power. My doctoral research looks closely at how people find real agency inside rigid systems: adapting imported components, reinterpreting a project's stated goals, quietly building the workaround that keeps a clinic running when the official process can't. I call this harnessing constraint, and it's the other half of the picture. Good localisation design has to build on that existing agency rather than design around it as if it isn't there.

My PhD examines the politics of localisation in Sub-Saharan Africa, focused on assistive technology and prosthetics and orthotics networks across East and West Africa.

Rather than treating localisation as an ethical promise or a donor checklist, I look at how it actually functions as governance in practice: a set of everyday administrative tasks, funding rules, and reporting routines that shape who holds real decision-making power, regardless of what the strategy document says.

The research traces this across four levels, from global donors and multilateral agencies, through intermediary NGOs and research hubs, down to frontline clinics and the technicians and patients navigating them day to day.

I bridge this with field experience across The Gambia, Uganda, Kenya, and Tanzania, and translate it into evaluation methodology, outcomes design, and strategic advice for the organisations actually doing this work.

Localisation Theatre
Following Femke Mulder: the performance of participation, consultation, and local sign-off, staged to satisfy donor compliance, while the funding, the timeline, and the real decisions stay exactly where they were.
Governance-in-Practice
Localisation understood not as a policy commitment but as the accumulation of ordinary administrative tasks, funding rules, and reporting routines that quietly decide who actually holds power.
Harnessing Constraint
The small, repeated adaptations through which local actors work inside donor-imposed limits: reinterpreting a brief, adjusting an imported part, building the workaround that keeps a clinic open.
Project Logic vs. Systemic Coherence
The gap between individual interventions that each look successful in isolation, and a wider system that never stabilises, because funding cycles, leadership, and reporting requirements don't talk to one another.

These terms are collected, alongside answers to the questions I get asked most, on the glossary.

Building On

Anthony Giddens, structuration: the same rules that constrain action are also what enable it, so structure and agency are produced together rather than opposed.
Femke Mulder, on localisation as performance: naming participatory process as staged compliance rather than an actual transfer of power.
Séverine Autesserre, on the meta-relationships that quietly decide which interventions are considered credible, and on finding real hope in the unglamorous, local relationships that international actors usually overlook.

Reading & Listening

Frontlines of Peace, Séverine Autesserre. Already in the lineage above. Worth reading for the cases rather than the theory. The chapter on Idjwi is the clearest argument I know for why outside intervention often works best when it does less.
How to Lose a Country, Ece Temelkuran. Written from Turkey, on democratic collapse as a sequence of unremarkable procedural steps rather than a single event. The procedural part is what programme design usually fails to see coming.
Shadowplay, Tim Marshall. I've read most of his geopolitics books. Fun, and more deterministic than they let on. This one is the outlier. He was Sky's diplomatic editor and spent the 1999 Kosovo crisis in Belgrade, so it's first-hand rather than an argument drawn on a map.
The Horn, International Crisis Group. Podcast. Keeps the regional reading current rather than three years out of date.
The Timbuktu School for Nomads, Nicholas Jubber. Travel writing across the Sahara, read with the usual reservations about the form. Good on the practical knowledge pastoralist life actually runs on, which rarely survives contact with a logframe.
AIL Español, B1. Classes twice a week. Still slow.
Jupiter's Travels, Ted Simon. Ted Simon rode a Triumph around the world for four years in the 1970s. All this does is feed the plan to ride from the UK down to South Africa.

Field Note

A new prosthetics and orthotics clinic in Bansang, The Gambia, opened with proper ramps and wide doors, exactly what accessible design guidance asks for. The path leading to it was steep, rocky, and unpaved. The hospital CEO explained that the funding covered the building itself; the ramps and the clinic were in scope, the path wasn't.

"It wasn't said defensively. It was just a fact."

The regional governor's office was sympathetic but realistic: the most likely route to a funded path ran through the British High Commission, the UN, or the EU lobbying central government to release money. To put concrete on a path. That's what governance-in-practice actually looks like on the ground, not a failure of goodwill, a question of who gets to decide what counts as "in scope."

The Waste End Once prosthetic components are flagged as clinically expired, British taxpayers pay roughly £369 per tonne to dispose of medical-grade titanium and carbon fibre. Functional hardware becomes waste because the regulatory script says so. Meanwhile a clinician in a Sub-Saharan workshop is scraping together $1,000 to $3,000 for whatever components they can get hold of.
The Workaround A pilot in The Gambia ran short of specialised silicone liners. Through the formal route that means a grant amendment, justification forms, and a stalled timeline. Instead I phoned a supplier chief executive I had met once at a conference two years earlier. He committed the liners inside a minute, and added a quarter more as a buffer. That call appears in no process document anywhere.

Independence

Where I stand, declared up front.

I hold a salaried role at a UK INGO. Any engagement arising from that organisation’s relationships is declared before a proposal is written.

Research published on this site uses organisations’ own public documents. Every organisation named in a study is sent its entry and given four weeks to correct the record before publication.
